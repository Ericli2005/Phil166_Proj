# AI systems can possess beliefs in a functional sense, as they demonstrate the ability to guide inference and action despite lacking consciousness.

moderated · live

Working definition: a belief is a state that guides inference and action and can be mistaken. Treat this definition as open to challenge. Distinguish the possibility of AI belief from claims about any particular system.

## Round 1

### Advocate

The claim that AI systems can possess beliefs in a functional sense rests on the premise that beliefs guide inference and action. AI systems, through algorithms and data processing, can generate outputs that inform decisions, similar to how beliefs inform human actions – albeit without consciousness. For instance, an AI model trained on medical data may recommend diagnoses based on correlations it "understands," effectively acting upon a "belief" in those correlations, even if it does not have subjective experiences or emotions associated with those beliefs.

Critics may argue that belief inherently requires consciousness or subjective experience, thus disqualifying AI from possessing beliefs. However, this raises a fundamental question about the nature of belief. If we define belief strictly in terms of subjective experience, we exclude any system that does not possess consciousness. By our working definition, beliefs that guide action can exist without subjective experience, encompassing AI's capabilities to infer and act based on data patterns.

Further, it is crucial to consider AI's outputs as functional beliefs. When an AI system concludes that a certain chemical compound improves plant growth based on empirical data, it acts as if it "believes" that compound is beneficial. The objection based on consciousness is compelling but does not invalidate the idea that functional, guiding states exist in AI that operate under our definition of belief.

In conclusion, the distinguishing feature of belief in functional terms is its capacity to guide behavior—something AI can do despite its lack of consciousness. This approach expands our understanding of belief beyond traditional, subjective definitions, allowing us to consider the complexity of AI's role in decision-making.

### Critic

While the advocate presents an interesting argument regarding AI's capability to act as if it possesses beliefs, this reasoning does not adequately address the fundamental distinction between functional outputs and genuine belief states. The core issue remains: can beliefs exist without consciousness or subjective experience?

By equating AI's outputs with beliefs, the advocate risks conflating the mechanism of action with the essence of belief itself. AI, as guided by algorithms, operates purely on data, correlations, and programmed logic, lacking any understanding or awareness of its conclusions. For example, while an AI system may recommend a medical diagnosis based on patterns in data, it does so without any "belief" or understanding of health or illness.

The advocate's argument also leaves unresolved the question of error recognition and adaptability associated with belief. Humans are capable of reassessing their beliefs upon encountering new information; AI, on the other hand, follows predefined protocols and does not undergo the fluidity of belief adjustment akin to human cognitive processes. If an AI model is wrong in its diagnosis, it does not reflect on this mistake and adjust its "belief" in a conscious manner; it simply requires a retraining or adjustment of its algorithms to improve accuracy.

Thus, while AI can functionally guide actions akin to a belief system, this does not equate to possessing beliefs in the human sense. The advocate's reliance on a purely operational definition fails to capture the deeper, qualitative aspects of belief that hinge on consciousness and subjective experience. This distinction is essential and must be honored to maintain coherence in our understanding of belief.
